Google Analytics Says We're Getting Conversions. But Are We Actually Making Money?

How I connected paid advertising data with CRM outcomes using SQL Server — and why the most interesting answers were hiding beyond the conversion numbers.

SQL ServerWindsor.aiCRM integrationPower BI & DAXMarketing analytics
Just because an analytics platform says someone has converted, does that actually mean they've brought any value to the business?
A little context first

Who I am, and why I ended up asking that question

I'm a data analyst who spent several years working in the UK car finance industry. My work involved supporting different parts of the business with management information, reporting and automation — often taking processes that relied on spreadsheets and turning them into something more reliable using SQL and Power BI.

Car finance is an interesting environment for a data analyst because a lead is only the beginning of the story. Someone can enquire about a car loan, but that doesn't mean they'll be approved, answer the phone, decide to proceed, sign their documents or ultimately take out the finance.

Those are very different outcomes, and they matter for different reasons. Marketing wants to know which activity brings people through the door. Sales wants to know which enquiries are worth following up. The business ultimately wants to understand which activity leads to completed deals.

That difference between getting an enquiry and getting a customer is what made this project so interesting to me.

The problem: we could see the marketing performance, but not the whole customer journey

We were running paid advertising through Google Ads and Microsoft Ads. Like most advertising platforms, they gave us plenty of information: spend, impressions, clicks, campaigns, keywords and conversions.

And that information was useful. But the conversion being reported by an advertising platform was generally much earlier in the journey than the outcome the business cared about. A submitted enquiry might count as a conversion even if the customer was later declined or never progressed.

Meanwhile, the CRM held a different set of answers. It recorded customer applications and the stages those applications reached — whether somebody had been contacted, showed interest, signed documents or completed the deal.

The problem was that these two worlds weren't naturally presented together. We could talk about advertising spend in one place and customer outcomes in another, but it was much harder to connect them consistently at the level needed to make decisions.

The question I wanted to answer:

Which advertising campaigns and keywords were bringing in customers who actually progressed through the sales pipeline — not just people who submitted an enquiry?

Death by spreadsheet

Before building a more joined-up approach, comparing marketing activity with CRM outcomes involved a lot of manual work. Export the advertising data. Export the CRM data. Put them into Excel. Try to match things up. Repeat when someone wanted a different date range or another breakdown.

Excel is brilliant for plenty of tasks, but when you're repeatedly stitching together datasets from different systems, it starts to feel like you're maintaining the process rather than analysing the results.

It also created practical problems. Different naming conventions, inconsistent fields, files getting larger, manual steps that were easy to miss, and a process that needed repeating whenever the data changed. Even when the numbers were right, it took too long to get to the interesting questions.

I didn't want another spreadsheet that needed rebuilding next week. I wanted a repeatable way to bring the data together so we could spend more time discussing what it meant.

Finding a solution — without replacing everything

The obvious question was whether we needed a different CRM or some all-in-one marketing platform. But replacing a core business system to solve a reporting problem would have been a pretty dramatic response.

We already had the information we needed; it was simply spread across different places. So rather than changing how the teams worked, I focused on connecting the data behind the scenes.

Windsor.ai provided a way to extract advertising performance from Google Ads and Microsoft Ads. I brought that information into SQL Server, where it could be stored, cleaned and prepared alongside the relevant CRM data.

Getting the data into a shape we could actually use

This is where the work became less about drawing a dashboard and more about understanding how the different datasets behaved.

First, the advertising platforms didn't speak exactly the same language

Google Ads and Microsoft Ads exposed similar information, but not always in identical formats. Even something as simple as demographic values could differ — for example, one source might use MALE and FEMALE, while another used M and F.

Those differences might look trivial when you're viewing one report at a time. They become much more important when you're trying to combine sources and compare them consistently.

I used SQL transformations and stored procedures to standardise fields, handle data types and prepare the advertising records for reporting. It meant the clean-up could happen as part of the process instead of being repeated manually every time.

Then came the identifiers — and a lesson I learnt the hard way

To connect advertising data to the CRM, it wasn't enough to have a campaign name that looked familiar. We needed the right identifiers from both sides, including the information captured when an enquiry entered the CRM.

At one stage, I realised I hadn't retained a campaign identifier that we would need later. Not my favourite discovery, but a useful one. A dataset can look perfectly reasonable until you ask it a question it was never designed to answer.

I went back, corrected the structure and made sure the identifiers were available in the ongoing process. It reinforced something I now pay much more attention to: think about the questions you need to answer before deciding which fields to keep.

Matching data is not the same as having perfect attribution

There was another important limitation. We didn't have a reliable click-level identifier, such as a GCLID, for every customer that could be traced directly back to an individual advertising click.

Instead, the reporting used the advertising and CRM identifiers available to us to compare outcomes at the relevant campaign and keyword level. That meant being careful about what the data could genuinely support. We could compare performance across matching groups, but we shouldn't pretend we had perfect click-by-click attribution where we didn't.

And because advertising spend and customer events are recorded at different levels of detail, I also had to be mindful of how the data was aggregated. Joining every event directly onto a spend row can make costs appear multiple times. Getting the grain right matters just as much as getting the join to run.

What happens after somebody becomes a lead?

This was the part I felt traditional advertising reporting was missing. A lead isn't a single final outcome; it's the beginning of a process.

For the analysis, we wanted to look at the stages that mattered to the business. An enquiry might progress through contact and interest, documents being signed and eventually a completed finance deal — or it might stop at any point along the way.

An illustrative customer journey

1,000 enquiries → 700 approved → 210 contacted and interested → 105 progressing further.

These numbers are illustrative, not actual company results. They demonstrate why counting enquiries alone doesn't tell the full story.

Once those outcomes could be compared with advertising spend, the questions changed. Instead of only asking about cost per lead, we could explore cost per approval, cost per interested customer, cost per signed agreement and cost per completed deal.

That is a very different conversation to simply celebrating a high number of conversions.

The campaign looked great. The keywords told a different story.

Once the SQL datasets were ready, I connected them to Power BI and used DAX to build measures around the different pipeline stages. We could move from an overall account view down to campaigns and then individual keywords, comparing spend with what happened to the customers those activities generated.

One of the most useful findings came from looking beneath the campaign totals. A campaign with roughly 10–20 keywords could appear to be performing well overall, but when we drilled into the detail, only two or three keywords were responsible for much of the valuable customer activity.

The others were still spending money. They could even be generating enquiries. But those enquiries weren't necessarily translating into the outcomes that mattered further down the pipeline.

Looking only at the whole campaign, £156.25 per completed deal might seem acceptable. But that average hides a huge difference between the keywords that are delivering customers and those that are mostly consuming budget.

That didn't automatically mean switching off every underperforming keyword. Context still mattered: volume, search intent, conversion delays and the role of each keyword within the wider campaign. But it gave the paid media team a much better starting point for reviewing bids, budgets and negative keywords.

And, perhaps most importantly, it opened up a conversation that wasn't possible from the advertising platform numbers alone.

From a daily reporting task to a place the paid media manager could actually use

One of the things I was most pleased with wasn't just the data pipeline itself. It was the management information we could build once the data was in the right place.

Before this, understanding campaign performance meant pulling information from different places, comparing advertising figures with CRM outcomes and spending time trying to piece together what was actually happening. That sort of work can become part of somebody's daily routine without anyone stopping to ask whether it really needs to be.

With the data prepared in SQL Server and connected to Power BI, the paid media manager had one central place to review performance whenever he needed it. He could start with the overall picture, drill into an advertising account, move down to a campaign and then look at the individual keywords behind the numbers.

The reports weren't just tables of figures, either. I used carefully colour-coded tables to make stronger and weaker performance easier to spot, alongside views showing trends and volumes over time. That made it much quicker to notice when a campaign was improving, when enquiries were increasing without the same growth in completed deals, or when a particular keyword deserved a closer look.

And because the reporting was built on a repeatable data process, he no longer had to carry out that same manual analysis every day just to get an up-to-date picture. Instead, he could open the report, explore what had changed and spend more time deciding what to do about it.

The real improvement:

Less time gathering and reconciling numbers. More time understanding performance, spotting opportunities and making informed decisions about campaigns and budgets.

For me, that's what good MI should do. It shouldn't just present information nicely; it should make the information easier to access, easier to interpret and genuinely useful to the person relying on it.

What did this actually achieve?

For me, this was the most important part of the project. Building a pipeline and writing SQL is satisfying, but neither means much if the result doesn't make somebody's job easier or help the business make better decisions. The real achievement was turning disconnected pieces of information into a reliable view of what our advertising spend was producing.

We stopped treating every enquiry as an equal result

Before we connected the data, it was easy to look at a campaign with lots of enquiries and assume it was doing well. But in car finance, an enquiry is only the start. A customer might be declined, never answer the phone, lose interest, sign their documents or eventually reach payout. Those are very different outcomes, even if they all began with the same marketing conversion.

By connecting advertising spend to the CRM pipeline, we could look at the cost of reaching meaningful stages rather than stopping at cost per lead. It became possible to compare campaigns and keywords on approvals, customer engagement, signed agreements and completed deals. That changed the questions we could ask — and, importantly, the answers we could give.

We could see where a campaign's performance was really coming from

One of the most useful discoveries came from drilling below the campaign level. A campaign could look perfectly healthy overall while only two or three of its keywords were responsible for most of its valuable customers. The remaining keywords might still be spending money and generating enquiries, but not producing the same results further down the funnel.

That gave the paid media manager a much stronger starting point for conversations about bids, budgets and negative keywords. Rather than relying on a broad campaign average, he could investigate exactly which parts of a campaign deserved more attention. It wasn't about the data making those decisions for him; it was about giving him the evidence to make them confidently.

The reporting became part of the workflow, not another daily task

There was also a very practical operational benefit. Previously, analysing performance meant going back and forth between platforms, CRM figures and spreadsheets, often repeating the same work the next day. Once the SQL Server process and Power BI reporting were in place, the paid media manager could open one central report whenever he needed an update.

He could drill from account to campaign to keyword, use colour-coded tables to spot strong and weak performers, and follow spend, enquiry volumes and customer outcomes over time. Instead of spending his time assembling the picture, he could spend it interpreting the picture. That's a meaningful improvement even before you put a financial value against it.

It made conversations between teams more useful

Marketing performance no longer had to be discussed purely in the language of clicks and conversions, while sales or operations looked at an entirely different set of numbers. Connecting those datasets gave us a shared way to discuss what happened to customers after they entered the business. It helped turn reporting into a conversation about the whole journey, from advertising spend through to a completed deal.

What I took away from it

The biggest achievement wasn't a single dashboard or a particular SQL technique. It was creating something repeatable that saved manual effort, made hidden performance differences visible and helped people ask better commercial questions. That's what I want my work in data to achieve.

I wouldn't claim the project automatically increased revenue or that every marketing decision became straightforward overnight. What I can say is that it gave the business a clearer, more accessible and more actionable understanding of its advertising performance — and removed a lot of the routine work needed to get there.

Why I'm not turning this into a 40-page SQL tutorial

There is a technical side to all of this: data extraction, schemas, stored procedures, transformation logic, matching identifiers, reporting models and DAX measures. I enjoy that side of the work, and it's essential to making the result dependable.

But I wanted this article to explain the thinking behind the project, not just list every line of code I wrote.

For me, modern analytics and data engineering isn't simply about knowing SQL syntax or being familiar with the latest platform. It's about understanding what you're working with, why a business needs it, how different systems connect and what can go wrong when you make assumptions about the data.

You can write a technically correct query that answers the wrong question. You can build a beautiful dashboard on top of a flawed relationship. And you can spend weeks automating a process without first asking whether it produces information anyone can use.

That's why I think the ability to translate between technical detail and business needs matters so much. The tools help you build the solution. Understanding the problem helps you build the right solution.

Final thoughts

This project started with a fairly simple frustration: we were being told advertising was generating conversions, but I wanted to understand what those conversions meant once they reached the business.

Following that question took me through multiple data sources, inconsistent fields, missing identifiers, CRM stages, SQL transformations and eventually keyword-level analysis that could change the way performance was discussed.

It also reminded me why I enjoy working with data. The interesting part isn't always the dashboard at the end. Sometimes it's the moment when two systems finally connect and you realise you can ask a question nobody had a reliable answer to before.

And in this case, that question was simple: are we generating more leads, or are we generating better customers?

This is a personal case study based on work in UK car finance. It describes the approach in general terms, omits confidential customer information and uses illustrative figures where examples are needed.
