Google Analytics Says We're Getting Conversions. But Are We Actually Making Money?

How I connected advertising performance to real business outcomes — and what it revealed about marketing spend.

SQL ServerPower BIDAXSnowflake recreation
Just because Google Analytics says someone has converted, does that mean they've brought any value to the business?

Clicks and enquiries are useful. But they don't tell you whether advertising spend is actually generating completed deals.

The business problem

The marketing team was investing in Google Ads and Microsoft Ads to generate new customers. At a campaign level, the numbers could look promising — but the real question was which campaigns and keywords produced customers who progressed through the sales pipeline.

Advertising data and CRM outcomes lived in separate places, making meaningful comparisons time-consuming and often spreadsheet-heavy.

Death by spreadsheet

Manually exporting reports, matching identifiers and comparing figures was not a sustainable way to measure performance. I wanted one reliable view of the full journey, from advertising click to completed deal.

The solution

I connected advertising performance from Google Ads and Microsoft Ads, via Windsor.ai, with CRM outcomes in SQL Server. SQL transformations and stored procedures prepared the data for analysis, while Power BI and DAX made it possible to explore performance at account, campaign and keyword level.

A key lesson was the importance of retaining the campaign identifiers needed for joining the datasets. Without those identifiers, reporting at the keyword level would not have been possible.

Recreating the pipeline in Snowflake

For this public portfolio, I'm rebuilding the data preparation side in Snowflake using fictional customer records and sample advertising data. The original business project used SQL Server and Power BI; this recreation focuses on a reusable, reporting-ready Snowflake dataset.

Raw data flows through staging checks into a reporting layer. Advertising spend and CRM outcomes must be aggregated to compatible grains before joining, to avoid duplicating costs.

The campaign looked great. The keywords told a different story.

Once the datasets were connected, I could drill from account to campaign to keyword and calculate meaningful measures: cost per application, approval, interested customer, signed agreement and completed deal.

In one real-world analysis, a campaign with roughly 10–20 keywords looked healthy overall, but only a handful were responsible for most of the valuable outcomes. The rest consumed budget without producing comparable results.

This insight gave the paid media team evidence to review bids, budgets and negative keywords based on real outcomes rather than surface-level conversion counts.

What did this achieve?

  • A clearer connection between advertising spend and business outcomes.
  • More meaningful campaign and keyword comparisons.
  • Visibility of where customers dropped out of the pipeline.
  • Better evidence for decisions about bidding and budget allocation.

Final thoughts

The biggest lesson was simple: an advertising conversion isn't the same thing as a business result. Connecting data across systems made it possible to see which marketing activity was genuinely valuable.

My Snowflake recreation makes that approach reproducible with fictional data, without exposing any customer or company information.
